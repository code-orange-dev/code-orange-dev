# Privacy Contributor Program Operating Standard

This standard turns the program architecture into an operating system. Its purpose is to protect upstream projects from performative student activity while making participant progression clear, fair, and evidence-based.

## 1. Admission and routing

Admission is competency-based, not attendance-based.

Every applicant completes a short diagnostic covering:

- Bitcoin transaction and privacy fundamentals;
- Git/GitHub workflow and local development setup;
- ability to build or navigate a relevant codebase;
- a written explanation of one code, protocol, or testing artifact;
- availability, learning goals, and collaboration expectations.

Applicants are routed to one of four destinations:

| Route | Entry condition | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| Foundations | Needs Bitcoin or development fundamentals | Bitcoin Dojo, rawBit, or self-study plan |
| OSS readiness | Has foundations but needs codebase workflow practice | Decoding Bitcoin / guided repository work |
| Privacy specialization | Can build, navigate, and communicate around scoped work | Privacy foundations and code labs |
| Direct rotation | Already shows equivalent privacy and OSS readiness | Project rotations after an orientation |

The diagnostic is not a prestige filter. Its job is to place people where they can succeed and to avoid using upstream repositories as beginner exercises.

## 2. Project intake and maintainer alignment

A project becomes an active rotation or residency target only after a short Project Readiness Brief is completed.

The brief records:

- active maintainer/contributor context and project contribution norms;
- the privacy or infrastructure area relevant to the program;
- build and test feasibility for participants;
- current issues, PRs, testing needs, documentation gaps, or research questions;
- language and prerequisite fit;
- whether meaningful review/validation work exists;
- likely mentor capacity and escalation path;
- risks: security sensitivity, unstable scope, or lack of upstream responsiveness.

Where practical, Code Orange asks an experienced upstream contributor about current priorities and whether structured learner involvement would be useful. This is a lightweight alignment check, not a request for maintainers to manage the cohort or grade students.

Projects are paused when they no longer have suitable work, become too sensitive for the cohort's current level, or lack enough context for useful participation.

## 3. Progression gates

Participants progress on demonstrated capability. A gate review is performed by a mentor using the scorecard and linked evidence.

### Gate A — Privacy specialization

Participant can explain a privacy threat model, build the relevant tools, and study an implementation or PR with guidance.

### Gate B — Rotation-ready

Participant can build an unfamiliar project, locate relevant code/tests, reproduce or validate a scoped change, and communicate evidence clearly.

### Gate C — Residency-ready

Participant has evidence across at least two contribution modes, including review or validation, and can propose a credible Contributor Plan for one project.

### Gate D — Fellowship-ready

Participant demonstrates sustained engagement, useful upstream interaction, growing independence, reliable communication, and a plausible case that funded time will increase their long-term project contribution.

Failure to clear a gate is not a failure of the participant. The result may be a targeted learning plan, additional rotation time, a different project, or a return to foundations.

## 4. Contributor Plan and portfolio

Each resident maintains a living Contributor Plan. It identifies one primary project, the relevant subsystem or problem, work already studied, a contribution mix, an upstream/mentor communication plan, risks, and a 90-day goal.

Each participant also maintains a private-by-default Contributor Portfolio. It links evidence rather than relying on mentor claims: test reports, review notes, issue investigations, patches, design write-ups, upstream discussion, and retrospective learning.

Participants choose what may be public. A public portfolio is encouraged where safe, but privacy and jurisdictional risk take precedence.

## 5. Review and feedback integrity

Review Club preparation is recorded even when no upstream comment is posted. Contributors are never asked to manufacture comments, approvals, or PRs for reporting purposes.

Mentors assess evidence against the scorecard. For residency and fellowship decisions, a second Code Orange reviewer calibrates the assessment when feasible. This reduces the risk that one mentor's preference becomes the whole program's judgement.

Upstream feedback is collected sparingly and treated as qualitative context. The preferred question is:

> Would you welcome this contributor continuing to work with the project? Why or why not?

No maintainer is asked to provide grades, allocate tasks, or make a funding decision.

## 6. Cohort reporting

Code Orange publishes an aggregate cohort report after the structured phase and updates retention at 3, 6, and 12 months. The report includes:

- the admissions and progression funnel;
- contribution mix by type;
- readiness, depth, and retention outcomes;
- examples of maintainer-aligned work;
- short contributor case studies where participants consent;
- lessons, project pauses, and changes made for the next cohort.

The report does not reduce the cohort to a single score or expose sensitive participant data.

## 7. Program governance and review

At the end of each cohort, the program lead and mentors review:

1. whether project choices reflected real upstream needs;
2. whether review/testing received equal instructional weight to authorship;
3. where participants stalled and whether routing or prerequisites need adjustment;
4. retention and upstream usefulness signals;
5. which curriculum modules should change based on active project work;
6. whether fellowship selections were consistent with the published criteria.

The outcome is a written cohort retrospective and a revised Project Readiness Brief list for the next intake.

## Non-negotiable principles

- Upstream projects are collaborators, not coursework props.
- No PR, review, or comment quota.
- Learning evidence and upstream contribution are reported separately.
- A difficult, slow, or abandoned technical investigation can still be valuable work.
- Participant safety and privacy outweigh public-reporting incentives.
- The standard of success is useful, sustained contribution - not the volume of visible GitHub activity.
